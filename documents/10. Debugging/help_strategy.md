# When to Use Google, AI Tools, or Ask for Help?

## Summary of Tasks

The work is documented in [test-repo-for-when-you-get-stuck-what-next](https://github.com/yjfvictor/test-repo-for-when-you-get-stuck-what-next). The tasks comprised researching best practices for troubleshooting coding problems (including a comparison of AI tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copilot, search engines, and consulting colleagues), developing a decision-making framework with a flowchart, and documenting the outcomes in that repository. The flowchart supports the choice of Google, AI tools, or colleagues according to problem complexity, information sensitivity, and urgency. The repository contains the flowchart in Mermaid format and guidance on how to use it.

## When I Prefer Using AI vs Searching Google

I prefer AI when I need a fast first pass on a clear, well-defined question, such as translating an error message, checking syntax or common patterns, or brainstorming possible causes. AI is useful when the problem fits a known pattern and I can verify the answer against documentation or my own code. I prefer Google (and sites like Stack Overflow) when I want to see multiple real-world solutions, compare different approaches, or need version-specific or framework-specific examples that are tied to actual discussions and code. So AI for speed and pattern matching, Google when I want variety and evidence from real developers.

## How I Decide When to Ask a Colleague Instead

I decide to ask a colleague when the problem is tied to our codebase, our architecture, or internal decisions that are not documented publicly. I also ask when I have been stuck for a while and AI or search have not helped, when I need to understand the reasoning behind a design or fix, or when the issue might involve sensitive or proprietary information that I should not put into external tools. If the task is urgent and blocking the team, I ask a colleague to unblock quickly rather than spending more time alone on the same approach.

## What Challenges Developers Face When Troubleshooting Alone

When troubleshooting alone, developers can waste time following wrong leads from AI or outdated search results. It is easy to miss mistakes in AI output when the topic is new, so they may apply incorrect fixes without realising. Without a colleague, there is no one to question assumptions or to share context about how the system actually works. Complex or multi-step bugs often need another pair of eyes to spot the real cause. Working alone can also make it harder to know when to stop searching and when to ask for help, which can lead to frustration and longer resolution times.
